A visual archive of how internet culture codified reality during COVID-19

Sunlight streams for us.

This is a story about GIFs during COVID-19. Before the pandemic, a GIF was a small joke. A reaction. Something you sent a friend to make them laugh. It described how we felt — but it wasn't how we felt.

The white boxes drown me in.

Then lockdown happened. We stopped seeing each other. GIFs stopped being optional. They became the fastest way to say I'm tired, I'm scared, I miss you — without needing words. The looping image carried what we couldn't.

The network is the message.

By mid-2020, something stranger had happened. GIFs weren't describing loneliness anymore. They were loneliness. The feeling and the image had collapsed into the same thing. This archive tracks that collapse — across 55 COVID-era keywords and 8 emotional districts of the internet.

Research Framework

Editorial Decisions

We chose Giphy's total_count as our primary metric because it represents the supply-side of cultural codification — how many GIFs the internet produced for a feeling, not how many times those GIFs were viewed. Supply reflects collective urgency: when millions of people independently create GIFs for 'loneliness', the act of creation is itself the signal.

The codification score (total_count / 500 × 100) is deliberately simple. We capped at 500 — the maximum Giphy returns per query — not because more GIFs don't exist, but because 500 already signals cultural saturation. The score is not a measure of emotion. It is a measure of how thoroughly a feeling has been processed into image.

55 keywords were selected through iterative refinement. We excluded political party names, brand names, and proper nouns — not because they weren't culturally significant, but because they conflate political identity with emotional expression. We were tracking feeling, not faction.

All API calls used lang=en. This is a significant editorial constraint: it renders the archive anglophone by default. COVID was not an anglophone experience. The data presented here is a window, not a mirror.

total_count is supply. We do not know how many times any given GIF was actually viewed, shared, or felt. The archive tells us what was made. What was received remains unmeasured.
