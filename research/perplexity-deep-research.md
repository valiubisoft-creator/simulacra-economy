## AREA 1: Theoretical foundation

### Baudrillard, simulacra, memes, hyperreality

**Political Media Memes as Simulacra in Media** — I.S. Kuznetsov — *Research in Social Sciences and Technology* (via DOAJ) — 2017
Key finding: Kuznetsov explicitly argues that media memes, especially political ones, function as Baudrillardian simulacra: sign-objects that circulate and gain autonomy in “media reality,” gradually replacing direct engagement with political processes and contributing to a post‑truth media sphere. Memes are treated as viral units whose evolution mirrors the stages of simulacra, suggesting that what people “know” about politics increasingly comes from memetic representations rather than events themselves.[^1][^2]
URL or DOI if available: https://doaj.org/article/b158f785bd274623955f131d6d8043f7[^2]

**Baudrillard’s Theory and the Hyperreal World of Social Media** — Armando Vieira — Personal essay / long‑form analysis — 2023
Key finding: Vieira synthesises Baudrillard for the contemporary social web, arguing that platforms like Instagram produce hyperreal identities where curated images, filters, and metrics (likes, followers) become more consequential for self‑worth than offline experience. Social feeds are framed as simulacra systems: users consume and perform signs of life (aestheticised photos, aspirational lifestyles) that detach from material conditions, effectively “replacing” reality as the primary reference for desire and social comparison.[^3]
URL or DOI if available: https://armandoscience.com/baudrillards-theory-and-the-hyperreal-world-of-social-media/[^3]

**Baudrillard’s Hyperreality and the Age of Instagram** — TheCollector editorial team — *TheCollector* — 2026
Key finding: This essay reads Instagram directly through Baudrillard, describing the platform as a “hyperreal” environment where filtered, staged images blur the line between authentic life and representation. It stresses how users orient their behaviour around images and markers (looking like influencers, performing lifestyle aesthetics), reinforcing the idea that social media does not merely mirror reality but supplies the dominant template through which reality is lived.[^4]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.thecollector.com/baudrillard-hyperreality-age-instagram/[^4]

**Memes as Cultural Texts: Semiotics, Symbolism, and Meaning** — (conference paper, author not clearly indicated in excerpt) — *Memes as Cultural Texts: Collective Identity and…* — ~2022
Key finding: This paper positions memes as multimodal signs (visual, textual, contextual layers) that compress complex cultural meaning into simple imagery and operate as “semiotic artefacts” performing identity work, ideological encoding, and collective commentary. Importantly for your project, it concludes that memes are both mirrors of the “emotional, ideological, and humorous pulse of society” and *molders* of societal narratives, explicitly acknowledging the feedback loop where memetic representations structure how events are remembered and felt.[^5]
URL or DOI if available: http://journalss.org/index.php/conf/article/download/11804/11362[^5]

**The Modes of Visual Rhetoric: Circulating Memes as Expressions** — (author not clearly visible in snippet) — Working paper / academic PDF — 2024
Key finding: This paper argues that to understand memes one must shift from analysing discrete “texts” to analysing *modes*—collective, emergent affective patterns that structure how image‑texts circulate and are felt. Using Fail/Win memes, it shows that memes express a broader “control society” logic, where participation in circulation and constant evaluation (fail/win) shapes subjectivity, suggesting that memes are not just reflections but operational logics that govern perception and behaviour.[^6]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.academia.edu/123950017/The_Modes_of_Visual_Rhetoric_Circulating_Memes_as_Expressions[^6]

### McLuhan’s “medium is the message” applied to platforms

**McLuhan’s Relevance in Today’s Society: A Look at Social Media on Mobile Devices** — Katherine Brubaker — Honors Thesis, Syracuse University — 2013
Key finding: Brubaker qualitatively studies Twitter and Flickr on mobile devices through McLuhan, concluding that “the medium is the message” is more explanatory than older “hot/cool” distinctions when analysing social feeds. She shows that always‑on, mobile access to streams changes temporal rhythms, attention, and expectations of constant connection, so the *form* (scrolling feed on a phone) becomes the real message structuring social life, more than any specific tweet or photo.[^7]
URL or DOI if available: https://surface.syr.edu/prl_thesis/2/[^7]

**How the Concepts of Marshall McLuhan Apply to Digital Platforms of Social Networks** — L. Morgado — *Comunicación y Hombre* — 2022
Key finding: Morgado explicitly re‑reads McLuhan in relation to Instagram, Twitter, and Facebook, describing social networks as “unavoidable wake‑up calls” that immerse users in a “false sense of seeing reality” through snippets of mediated content. The paper argues that social media’s message is the transformation of social time and attention into a continuous, global “village,” where what counts as reality is what appears in the feed, aligning neatly with your gaze‑economy framing.[^8]
URL or DOI if available: https://comunicacionyhombre.com/en/article/the-medium-is-the-message-and-social-networks-how-the-concepts-of-marshall-mcluhan-apply-to-digital-platforms-of-social-networks/[^8]

**Facebook Is the Message: McLuhan’s Theory Revisited** — article in *Research in Social Sciences and Technology* — ~2017
Key finding: This paper treats Facebook itself—not its posts—as McLuhan’s “message,” arguing that the structural features of the platform (timeline, likes, comments) reshape how users understand social relations and “real” community. It stresses that the social world experienced on Facebook is structurally different from offline life, yet becomes a primary arena where social identity is constructed, again suggesting substitution rather than mere mirroring.[^9]
URL or DOI if available: https://ressat.org/index.php/ressat/article/download/37/25/[^9]

### GIFs, memes, visual reflex, emotional shorthand

**Emoticons vs. Reaction GIFs: Non‑Verbal Communication on the Internet from the Aspects of Visuality, Verbality and Time** — Ágnes Veszelszki — In *Beyond Words. Pictures, Parables, Paradoxes* (Peter Lang) — 2015
Key finding: Veszelszki empirically studies reaction GIF usage and argues that reaction GIFs constitute a distinct form of non‑verbal communication with stronger emotional intensity than emoticons, due partly to their temporal (motion) dimension. Participants report that GIFs often require shared pop‑culture knowledge, turning them into compressed cultural codes that deliver affective “shortcuts” in conversation—very close to your idea of visual reflex.[^10]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.academia.edu/15173853/[^10]

**Digital Communication in a GIF Culture: The Graphic Interchange Format as a Behavioral Crossroad** — (author in snippet not fully shown) — 2015/2017
Key finding: This paper conceptualises GIFs as para‑ or pseudo‑linguistic units: self‑contained emotional statements whose origin or narrative context often becomes irrelevant to their current affective use. It argues that GIFs “state a perfectly eligible emotion” regardless of the original source, framing GIF culture as a system where recurrent visual loops are re‑coded into general emotional lexicons—essentially the codification process you want to visualise.[^11]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.academia.edu/40667384/Digital_communication_in_a_GIF_culture_the_Graphic_Interchange_Format_as_a_behavioral_crossroad[^11]

**Students’ Perceptions of Emojis, Memes and GIFs in Higher Education** — B. Hayes — *Student Engagement in Higher Education Journal* — 2024
Key finding: Hayes finds that students see emojis, memes and GIFs as useful tools for fostering “personability” of lecturers and aiding attention and understanding, while also noting risks of distraction and over‑disclosure. Here GIFs/memes function as emotional shorthand and relational signals—quick, recognisable gestures that stand in for more elaborate explanation—supporting the idea that they become standardised reflexes for feeling and social stance.[^12]
URL or DOI if available: https://sehej.raise-network.com/raise/article/view/1187[^12]

**Meme Templates as Expressive Repertoires in a Globalizing China** — Limor Shifman \& co‑authors — *Journal of Computer‑Mediated Communication* — 2018
Key finding: This paper treats meme templates as “expressive repertoires” embedding typical emotions and social identities, showing that global meme templates are dominated by Western pop culture but local variants emerge around specific events. It finds that meme emotions skew negative (anger, ironic happiness) and that templates offer users prefabricated emotional stances, again aligning with the idea that memes provide a set of ready‑made “reflex” responses to reality.[^13]
URL or DOI if available: https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/23/5/294/5075540[^13]

**Memes as Cultural Texts: Semiotics, Symbolism, and Meaning** — see above — ~2022
Key finding: Beyond the semiotics, this paper explicitly notes that memes “serve as both mirrors and molders of societal narratives,” archiving shared experiences through visual storytelling while simultaneously shaping which experiences become collectively salient. That dual role—archive *and* lens—goes straight to your core question of mirror versus constructor of reality.[^5]
URL or DOI if available: http://journalss.org/index.php/conf/article/download/11804/11362[^5]

## AREA 2: COVID‑19 as a cultural stress test

### Surge in memes/GIFs and quantified usage

**COVID‑19 in Memes: The Adaptive Response of Societies to the Pandemic** — P. Skórka et al. — *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* — 2022
Key finding: Using Google and Google Trends, Skórka et al. show that searches and online interest in “coronavirus memes” exploded during the spring 2020 lockdown, with the temporal pattern of “covid meme” interest positively correlated with new case counts and death numbers globally. They interpret this as evidence that societies turned to humorous COVID memes as an adaptive coping response, with meme intensity peaking in the early lockdown and then declining, mirroring the epidemiological curve.[^14]
URL or DOI if available: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph191912338[^14]

**How Covid‑19 Has Altered Searches on Giphy** — Adweek (reporting Giphy internal data) — 2020
Key finding: Giphy reported that usage of its GIF platform rose 33% over the month prior to April 8, 2020, attributing the spike to increased reliance on GIFs for digital expression during social distancing. The company described Giphy data as “a barometer for how people are feeling,” noting large increases in searches for “coronavirus” alongside positivity and encouragement themes.[^15]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.adweek.com/performance-marketing/how-covid-19-has-altered-searches-on-giphy/[^15]

**Giphy Usage Continues to Grow During Coronavirus Pandemic** — Adweek — 2020
Key finding: A follow‑up article noted that Giphy usage in April 2020 was up 13% versus March and 65% versus February, with the Giphy Cares channel (for supportive GIFs) reaching 1.6 billion views. The report frames GIFs as closing “the gaps caused by social distancing,” supporting your intuition that GIF culture became a primary vehicle for emotional communication when physical contact collapsed.[^16]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.adweek.com/performance-marketing/giphy-usage-continues-to-grow-during-coronavirus-pandemic/[^16]

**Impact of the COVID‑19 Pandemic on Social Media** — Wikipedia (synthesising multiple studies and platform reports) — 2020–2023
Key finding: This overview notes a 10.5% increase in active social media users from July 2019 to 2020, a 70% surge in Instagram Live viewership in early lockdown, and a 48.3% increase in TikTok unique visitors from January to March 2020. It also documents the explosion of COVID meme groups such as “Zoom Memes for Self Quaranteens,” which quickly reached over 500,000 members, indicating memetic codification of everyday quarantine experience.[^17]
URL or DOI if available: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_of_the_COVID-19_pandemic_on_social_media[^17]

### Doomscrolling as named phenomenon

**UF Study Finds ‘Doomscrolling’ Is a Unique Behavior** — University of Florida News — 2022
Key finding: Reporting on a study in APA’s *Technology, Mind, and Behavior*, UF researchers describe doomscrolling as a distinct behaviour characterised by compulsive consumption of negative news online, not reducible to general anxiety or media use. The article notes that the term likely originated on Twitter around 2018, but gained wide traction in 2020 during COVID, and that doomscrolling is associated with fear of missing out and constant smartphone connectivity.[^18]
URL or DOI if available: https://news.ufl.edu/2022/01/doomscrolling/[^18]

**Doomscrolling Scale: Its Association with Personality Traits, Social Media Use, and Well‑Being** — M. Kircaburun et al. — *International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction* — 2022
Key finding: This paper develops a multi‑item Doomscrolling Scale and, across multiple studies, finds doomscrolling positively correlated with neuroticism, social media addiction, fear of missing out, daily hours of social media, and psychological distress. Structural equation modelling suggests doomscrolling has significant negative relationships with well‑being indicators, mediated by distress, supporting the idea that the behaviour intensifies negative affect rather than just reflecting pre‑existing conditions.[^19]
URL or DOI if available: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11469-022-00809-0[^19]

### Passive use, increased time online, but decreased meaningful connection

**Facing Loneliness and Anxiety During the COVID‑19 Isolation: The Role of Excessive Social Media Use** — A. Marazziti et al. — *Frontiers in Psychiatry* — 2020
Key finding: In an Italian adult sample, perceived loneliness predicted both excessive social media use and anxiety, and excessive use in turn predicted increased anxiety, suggesting a vicious cycle. The authors interpret this as people turning to social media to cope with isolation, but the ease and duration of access during lockdown risked *worsening* anxiety rather than relieving it—an early empirical articulation of “internet as cage.”[^20]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.586222/full[^20]

**Loneliness and Its Association With Social Media Use During the COVID‑19 Outbreak** — R. Hansen et al. — Preprint/Journal article (multi‑country sample) — 2021
Key finding: This study finds that high‑frequency social media use after the outbreak is associated with poorer mental and psychosocial health overall, but that patterns vary by age: more frequent use of multiple platforms is linked to *lower* social loneliness among the oldest participants yet *higher* emotional loneliness among the youngest. Video‑based communication is associated with less loneliness and higher quality of life in 60–69‑year‑olds, suggesting a partial compensatory function that does not extend equally across age groups.[^21]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.academia.edu/52288368/Loneliness_and_Its_Association_With_Social_Media_Use_During_the_COVID_19_Outbreak[^21]

**Social Media Use and Loneliness During the COVID‑19 Pandemic** — E. Baker — Master’s Thesis, Georgia Southern University — 2021
Key finding: This thesis reviews evidence that social media became more integrated into daily life during the pandemic and hypothesises that increased social media activity could reduce loneliness by facilitating connection when face‑to‑face contact is impossible. Preliminary analyses suggest nuanced effects: some users may experience reduced loneliness through online ties, while others see no benefit or even increased distress, depending on how platforms are used.[^22]
URL or DOI if available: https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/etd/2605/[^22]

*Contradiction:* Across these studies, social media is simultaneously a coping tool and a risk factor: video‑based, active use may help certain populations (older adults), while high‑frequency or excessive, more passive use is linked to anxiety and poorer well‑being, especially among younger users.[^20][^22][^21]

### COVID memes as coping mechanisms

**Internet Memes Related to the COVID‑19 Pandemic as a Potential Coping Mechanism for Anxiety** — M. Akram et al. — *Psychology of Popular Media* (via PubMed) — 2021
Key finding: In an experiment with 80 clinically anxious and 80 non‑anxious participants, anxious individuals rated COVID‑19 memes as more humorous, relatable, and shareable than non‑anxious controls, while emotion‑regulation difficulties did not mediate this effect. The authors suggest that COVID‑related memes may serve as a tentative coping mechanism for people with severe anxiety symptoms by offering humour and shared recognition of distress.[^23]
URL or DOI if available: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34772957/[^23]

**COVID‑19 in Memes: The Adaptive Response of Societies to the Pandemic** — Skórka et al. — see above — 2022
Key finding: In addition to documenting the surge in COVID memes, Skórka et al. find that COVID memes are rated as funnier than non‑COVID memes despite similar proportions of positive/negative words, which they interpret as evidence that dark humour around the pandemic functioned as adaptive coping. The short lifespan and rapid turnover of memes is taken as a sign that collective attention was working through acute stressors in compressed, memetic bursts.[^14]
URL or DOI if available: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph191912338[^14]

### Correlation of COVID events with meme/GIF activity

**COVID‑19 in Memes** — Skórka et al. — see above — 2022
Key finding: Time‑series analysis shows that Google search interest in “covid meme” is positively correlated with temporal changes in new COVID case counts ($r = 0.369$) and deaths ($r = 0.640$) across weeks. Interest peaks during the first lockdown, suggesting that meme production and consumption spike when threat salience and uncertainty are highest.[^14]
URL or DOI if available: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph191912338[^14]

**COVID‑19 Related Sentiment Analysis Using State‑of‑the‑Art Machine Learning and Deep Learning Models** — Z. Jalil et al. — *Frontiers in Public Health* — 2022
Key finding: Using multiple Twitter datasets (COVIDSenti A/B/C), the authors show that COVID‑related tweets skew negative overall, with frequent keywords including “Coronavirus,” “COVID‑19,” “virus,” “social distance,” “lockdown,” and “stay home,” and track sentiment trends over time. While not meme‑specific, their keyword trend analysis can be aligned with meme/GIF query volumes to test whether spikes in specific event‑related terms (e.g., “lockdown”, “stay home”) correspond to GIF search surges.[^24]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2021.812735/full[^24]

Key finding: Although primarily focused on sentiment polarity, the authors note that the most frequent keywords cluster around fear of disease, enforcement of social distance, and stay‑at‑home mandates, with negative sentiment dominating early lockdown tweets. URL or DOI if available: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2021.812735/full[^24]

**Facing Loneliness and Anxiety During COVID‑19 Isolation** — Marazziti et al. — see above — 2020
Key finding: The psychometric scales in this study centre on loneliness and anxiety, suggesting “loneliness,” “anxiety,” “social media use,” “excessive use,” and “addictive‑like use” as key psychological constructs to track in social media language. They don’t provide a Twitter keyword list, but their conceptual vocabulary maps nicely to tags like \#lonely, \#anxiety, \#panic, \#worried.[^20]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.586222/full[^20]

**Proposed emotional keyword categories (based on these studies)**

*Emotional / psychological:*

- loneliness, lonely, isolation, isolated, anxiety, anxious, depression, depressed, stress, stressed, fear, afraid, panic, hopeless, nervous, distressed, burnout.[^32][^30][^20]

*Meta‑digital / experiential:*

- doomscrolling, zoom fatigue, quarantine boredom, cabin fever, pandemic fatigue, FOMO (fear of missing out).[^18][^19][^17]

*Well‑being / coping:*

- self‑care, cope, coping, survive, \#MyPandemicSurvivalPlan, prayer, gratitude, support, mental health.[^30][^32][^29]

In terms of “ranked by volume,” Chen’s filtered mental‑health dataset and Jalil’s frequency counts provide the clearest quantitative signal for which emotional terms (e.g., anxiety, depression, lonely) dominate.[^30][^24]

## AREA 4: Internet as cage vs. respite — social alienation

### Internet as respite / partial antidote to isolation

**How COVID‑19 Changed Self‑Presentation on Instagram and What This Tells Us About Social Media Use and Loneliness** — M. Turner — *Interacting with Computers* — 2023
Key finding: Turner notes that visits to Instagram increased by 43% in 2020 compared to 2019 and argues that people used social media to compensate for reduced access to normal support networks and to mitigate anxiety during lockdown. However, the paper also highlights that using social media for pandemic news and social comparison can produce negative consequences, making Instagram both a support structure and a stressor.[^33]
URL or DOI if available: https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article/35/5/590/7050891[^33]

**Loneliness and Its Association With Social Media Use During the COVID‑19 Outbreak** — Hansen et al. — see above — 2021
Key finding: Older adults (60–69) who used more types of social media and video‑based communication reported *lower* social loneliness and higher quality of life, suggesting that digital communication helped them maintain bonds during isolation. In contrast, younger high‑frequency users experienced higher emotional loneliness and poorer mental health, indicating that the same tools can be respite for some and a trap for others.[^21]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.academia.edu/52288368/Loneliness_and_Its_Association_With_Social_Media_Use_During_the_COVID_19_Outbreak[^21]

**Face‑to‑Face More Important Than Digital Communication for Mental Health, but Digital Still Matters** — S. Stieger et al. — *Scientific Reports* — 2023
Key finding: In a large survey, both face‑to‑face and digital text communication were significant predictors of better mental health during lockdown, with face‑to‑face being stronger but digital still meaningful. More “life‑like” communication modes (video calls) were associated with less loneliness and more happiness, yet they remained inferior substitutes to in‑person interaction, reinforcing a hierarchy of media.[^34]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-34957-4[^34]

**Social Media Discussion on COVID‑19 Impact on Mental Health** — Chen — see above — 2022
Key finding: The thesis documents that many COVID mental‑health tweets involve seeking and offering support, normalising distress, and sharing coping strategies, indicating that social media served as a public forum for collective sense‑making and peer support. At the same time, high levels of negative sentiment and recurring themes of anxiety and fear show that this supportive use co‑exists with distress narration.[^30]
URL or DOI if available: https://digitalcommons.bryant.edu/honors_data_science/6/[^30]

**Scrolling TikTok to Soothe and Foster Self‑Care During the COVID‑19 Pandemic** — L. Abidin et al. — *Social Media + Society* — 2023
Key finding: This qualitative study shows that users framed “mindless” TikTok scrolling—normally seen as time‑wasting—as a form of self‑care and emotional regulation during the crisis, helping them “wait out” conditions beyond their control. Yet it also argues that such self‑soothing scrolls can distract from structural inequalities and act as self‑discipline, revealing a paradox where TikTok is both solace and soft cage.[^35]
URL or DOI if available: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20563051231213542[^35]

### Internet as cage / amplifier of alienation

**Passive Facebook Usage Undermines Affective Well‑Being: Experimental and Longitudinal Evidence** — H. Verduyn et al. — *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* — 2015
Key finding: Two studies show that experimentally cued *passive* Facebook use (browsing without interacting) leads to declines in affective well‑being—about a 9% drop by day’s end in one experiment—largely mediated by increased envy. Importantly, this effect holds when controlling for active use and other online/offline interactions, indicating that passively consuming others’ lives in feeds specifically undermines mood.[^36]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.academia.edu/77554430/Passive_Facebook_Usage_Undermines_Affective_Well_Being_Experimental_and_Longitudinal_Evidence[^36]

**The Epidemic of Loneliness: A 9‑Year Longitudinal Study of the Impact of Passive and Active Social Media Use on Loneliness** — J. Roberts et al. — *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* — 2024
Key finding: Reviewing prior work and longitudinal data, Roberts et al. find that social networking use is positively but weakly associated with loneliness overall, but note that *how* social media is used matters: passive use is closely associated with higher loneliness via upward social comparisons and displacement of face‑to‑face interaction. Persistent high levels of both passive and active engagement over years predict higher loneliness compared with marginal or no use.[^37]
URL or DOI if available: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01461672241295870[^37]

**Facing Loneliness and Anxiety During COVID‑19 Isolation** — Marazziti et al. — see above — 2020
Key finding: The study shows that loneliness drives excessive social media use, which in turn increases anxiety, suggesting that for some participants the platforms intensify rather than alleviate distress. This dynamic embodies the “internet as cage” model: the more anxious and lonely people feel, the more they scroll, and the more anxious they become.[^20]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.586222/full[^20]

**Face‑to‑Face More Important Than Digital Communication** — Stieger et al. — see above — 2023
Key finding: Stieger et al. explicitly conclude that technology‑mediated communication “is not capable to be a substitute for face‑to‑face communication,” with face‑to‑face contact being “the gold standard” for mental health. Even though digital contact helps somewhat, the clear gradient from face‑to‑face to video to text shows that digital communication tends to be a weaker, potentially unsatisfying replacement, especially under prolonged lockdowns.[^34]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-34957-4[^34]

**Social Networks, Social Support, and Mental Health** — J. Moore — Conference paper (Ohio Communication Association) — 2018
Key finding: Through interviews, Moore finds that while people express willingness to offer support to distressed friends online, they often feel reluctant or fatigued when contacts chronically post about stress or negative affect, leading to “curtailed communication” and “establishment of communication barriers.” This suggests a concept of *social liability*: the obligation to provide emotional support online can exhaust social resources and reduce support precisely when depressed users need it most.[^38]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.ohiocomm.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Moore-Manuscript.pdf[^38]

**The Relationship Between Social Comparison and Depressive Symptoms Among Indonesian Instagram Users** — A. B. Mutiara — *Advances in Social Science, Education and Humanities Research* — 2018
Key finding: Mutiara finds that negative social comparison on Instagram is significantly associated with depressive symptoms, and that following more strangers increases social comparison even when overall time spent on the app does not. This aligns with other work (e.g., Lup et al.) showing that platforms oriented around aesthetic display and metrics can foster depressive affect through constant comparison.[^39]
URL or DOI if available: https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/25899597.pdf[^39]

*Contradictions and tensions:*

- For older users and in video‑based, active communication modes, platforms can reduce loneliness and support mental health, functioning as a lifeline during lockdown.[^21][^34]
- For younger and high‑frequency users, passive consumption and comparison are consistently linked to increased loneliness, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, especially when offline contact is constrained.[^37][^36][^39][^20]
- Qualitative TikTok work shows people framing scrolling as self‑care, yet critical analysis suggests it also disciplines users into self‑management and distracts from structural conditions, embodying the cage/respite duality in one behaviour.[^35]

**Winning the Internet** — The Pudding — Data‑driven essay — 2020
Key finding: This project analyses which links and content “win” viral attention, using large‑scale newsletter and social data to visualise patterns of what gets shared and why. Formally, it uses scrollytelling, network diagrams, and interactive filtering to show how small variations in framing, thumbnail, or topic affect attention, aligning with your gaze‑economy concerns even though it’s not meme‑specific.[^40]
URL or DOI if available: https://github.com/the-pudding/data (datasets) and https://pudding.cool (story)[^40]

**World Data Visualization Prize Entries (Attention‑Economy Adjacent)** — Various creators — *World Government Summit / Information is Beautiful* — 2019
Key finding: While focused on governance, the winning interactive piece “GOV | DNA” visualises the “DNA of good government” with multi‑indicator comparisons and interactive exploration.[^41] Its relevance for you is methodological: it demonstrates how to encode multi‑dimensional attributes (e.g., meme density, emotional valence, event proximity) into a compositional “DNA”‑like glyph that can represent the codification of a concept.[^41]
URL or DOI if available: https://informationisbeautiful.net/2019/winners-of-the-world-data-visualization-prize/[^41]

### Net art, surveillance, hyperreality, connectivity

**Tracking Transience** — Hasan Elahi — Net art / surveillance project — 2002–ongoing
Key finding: After being mistakenly placed on a terror watchlist, Elahi began publishing exhaustive details of his life—locations, meals, beds, toilets—online, turning himself into a voluntarily monitored hyperreal data stream. Exhibited in shows like *Watched! Surveillance, Art and Photography*, the work flips the surveillance gaze back onto itself and prefigures social media oversharing as a strategy for both control and self‑performance, resonating with Baudrillard’s hyperreal subject who exists as data.[^42]
URL or DOI if available: Article overview: https://crackmagazine.net/article/long-reads/beyond-big-brother-co-berlins-exhibition-asks-are-we-paranoid-enough-about-the-reality-of-modern-surveillance/[^42]

**Controlling_Connectivity** — Gretta Louw — Durational performance / net art — 2011 (book and essays later)
Key finding: Louw lived in a gallery space in complete physical isolation, interacting only via internet‑based social networking, as a “severe and systematic disruption of normal, socially accepted patterns of life.” The project foregrounds the psychological burden and obligation of constant connectivity, making explicit the feeling of the internet as both lifeline and cage that your project is thematising.[^43]
URL or DOI if available: Overview: https://deeannamerznagel.com/controlling_connectivity-art-media-and-internet-addiction/[^43]

**Today’s Top 25 Expiring Internet Artists** — Guido Segni — Net art — 2013–ongoing
Key finding: This site lists “Top 25 expiring internet artists,” playing with domain expiration dates to foreground the hyper‑competitive, anxiety‑ridden nature of online artistic presence. The work uses simple tabular data and time as its main visual axis, pointing to the precariousness and metrics‑driven nature of online visibility—conceptually close to an attention economy for artists.[^44]
URL or DOI if available: Contextual overview: https://amt-lab.org/blog/2020/12/net-art-a-reflection-of-current-digital-material-conditions[^44]

### Meme / GIF culture as data (closest precedents)

Key finding: The developer docs describe endpoints for search, trending, translation, and random GIFs, providing query parameters like `q` (keyword), `limit`, `offset`, and rating filters. This confirms that your proposed approach—probing the “codification” of concepts via Giphy search density—is technically aligned with how Giphy itself organises GIF culture as an addressable database.[^45]

Key finding: While not a data vis project, this paper treats GIFs explicitly as a cultural corpus, using empirical research on reaction‑GIF usage to describe them as behavioural crossroads and emotional statements. For your purposes, it provides conceptual justification for treating individual GIF search results as units of emotional/cultural codification in a data visualisation.[^11]

Key finding: This essay surveys net art projects that critique platform capitalism, hypercompetition, and anxiety, such as Segni’s “Top Expiring Internet Artists,” framing them as responses to hegemonic internet infrastructures. ## How this feeds your central question (mirror vs constructor, and measurability)

Across these sources, a few analytically useful tensions emerge:

- **Mirror vs constructor:** Meme scholarship and Baudrillard‑inspired analyses repeatedly insist that memes and platforms both *reflect* and *mould* reality—memes are “mirrors and molders” of narratives, meme templates offer prefabricated emotional repertoires, and social media feeds give a “false sense of seeing reality” via snippets that become lived reference points.[^8][^13][^4][^5]
- **GIFs/memes as visual reflex:** GIF culture research shows that reaction GIFs function as pseudo‑linguistic emotional statements that compress complex emotions into instantly recognisable loops, which is exactly the codification you want to measure; in practice, users reach for a GIF or meme instead of verbalising experience.[^12][^10][^11]
- **COVID as stress test:** Quantitative work links spikes in COVID cases and death counts with spikes in “covid meme” searches, increases in GIF platform usage, and surges in negative sentiment Twitter chatter, while experimental work demonstrates that anxious individuals find COVID memes particularly humorous and shareable.[^23][^16][^15][^14][^24]
- **Alienation vs connection:** Longitudinal and experimental studies show that passive, comparison‑based use and doomscrolling are associated with increased loneliness, depression and anxiety, while active, video‑based communication can reduce loneliness for some groups, especially older adults.[^19][^36][^39][^37][^34][^21]

From a data‑viz angle, these contradictions are actually gold: you can design visual encodings that explicitly show, for example, that the same keyword (e.g., “loneliness”) is associated with both supportive, community‑oriented GIFs and with doomscrolling contexts, or that periods of high meme codification (dense Giphy results) coincide with both peaks in pandemic threat and in online coping discourse.

If you’d like, next step could be: designing a concrete Giphy‑query schema + a few prototype p5.js sketch ideas that encode “codification density × emotional valence × epidemiological timeline” as a visual system.
